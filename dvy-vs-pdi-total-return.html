$DVY vs $PDI: Total Return, Income and Which Is Better in 2026

By Benjie · November 19, 2026 · 11 min read

Sponsored By IncomeBlastETFs.com

Over the last 10 years, BlackRock's $DVY has beaten PIMCO's $PDI on total return: 9.76% a year at NAV for $DVY (through September 30, 2026) versus 8.72% at NAV and 8.21% at market price for $PDI (through June 30, 2026). $PDI pays far more income, about 19% at today's price versus about 3.3% for $DVY, but most of that extra yield has been given back in price.

"$DVY vs $PDI" has been showing up in the searches that land on this site, and honestly, it's a strange matchup at first glance. One is a plain iShares stock ETF full of banks and utilities. The other is a leveraged PIMCO bond fund that trades like a stock. But I get why people line them up. Both get bought for income. And the question underneath is always the same: is the giant yield worth it?

Let's answer it the way this site always does. Total return: price change plus every distribution, reinvested. Straight from iShares, PIMCO's own SEC filings and the closed-end fund data on CEF Connect.

The 5 year row is the one that jumps out. $DVY did almost 9.7% a year. $PDI did 6.2% at NAV and just 3.8% at the price you'd actually have paid and sold at. That gap between NAV and market price is the story of this fund, and I'll get to it.

Between those dates, $PDI paid five monthly distributions of $0.2205, or $1.10 a share. Add that back and my rough math says the NAV total return was about -2.6% and the market price total return was about -10.4% in under five months. Not a disaster at NAV. But if you bought at a premium in the summer, it stung.

Two things happened at once. The NAV slid, and the premium people were paying for $PDI flipped to a discount. CEF Connect shows a 52 week average premium of 3.78% and a 52 week high of 12.32%, and now it trades below what its bonds are worth. That's exactly why $PDI's market price returns lag its NAV returns. When the premium shrinks, shareholders lose on top of whatever the bonds do.

And that 19% distribution rate? It's high mostly because the price dropped. The check didn't get bigger.

What's the difference between $DVY and $PDI?

$DVY is the iShares Select Dividend ETF, launched in November 2003. It tracks the Dow Jones U.S. Select Dividend Index: about 100 of the higher yielding U.S. stocks that pass screens on dividend history and payout, weighted by the dividends they pay. It held 98 stocks on November 18, 2026. It's an ETF, so it trades almost exactly at NAV (iShares showed a -0.01% discount that day).

$PDI is the PIMCO Dynamic Income Fund, a closed-end fund that started in May 2012. It's actively managed, it's mostly bonds and loans, and it uses a lot of borrowed money. Because it's closed-end, the share count is fixed day to day, so the price can drift well above or below NAV depending on how badly people want in.

What's actually inside each fund

$DVY is a stock fund that leans hard into old school income sectors. On iShares' numbers from mid November, financials were about 25% of the fund and utilities about 23%, then consumer staples around 14% and energy around 10%. Tech was only about 3%. Top names include Altria, Pfizer, Prudential Financial, HP and T. Rowe Price, and no single stock was even 2.5%. iShares lists a 3 year equity beta of 0.49, so it has moved about half as much as the market.

$PDI is a credit fund. Here's how PIMCO's annual report broke it down on June 30, 2026, as a share of investments:

So with $DVY you own slices of real businesses whose dividends can grow. With $PDI you own riskier credit (mortgages without a government guarantee, corporate loans, high yield bonds) and then PIMCO borrows on top of it to juice the income. When credit is calm, that's a money machine. When credit gets nervous, the leverage works in reverse.

Income: 3% that grows vs 19% that doesn't

On $100,000, here's the rough math today:

  • $DVY: about $3,300 a year at its 3.29% trailing yield, paid quarterly. Over the last year it paid about $5.34 a share, and the payout grew roughly 7% a year from 2021 to 2025, according to the dividend history on my sister site DividendProjection.com.
  • $PDI: about $19,000 a year at the current $13.86 price, paid monthly. The distribution has been exactly $0.2205 every month in the payment record I checked, going back to April 2025.

That's a huge difference in cash. But here's how I actually think about it. $PDI's check has been flat, while its NAV fell from $15.93 to $14.42 just since June. $DVY's check has been growing. If you spend the income, $PDI pays you a lot more today. If you reinvest, look at the total return table again, because that's what you actually keep.

Taxes matter here too. $DVY's dividends come from U.S. companies and are mostly qualified, which means lower tax rates in a taxable account. $PDI's distributions come mostly from bond interest, which is generally taxed as ordinary income. PIMCO also notes that distributions can include return of capital, and it reports the estimated sources in Section 19 notices. Check your 1099 and talk to a tax pro about your situation.

If monthly income is the goal, MonthlyETFs.com tracks the funds that pay every month, and TopDividendETFs.com ranks the big dividend funds with price decay flags.

Fees: 0.38% vs 3.79%

This is the number that surprises people. PIMCO's annual report estimates $PDI's total annual expenses at 3.79% of common share net assets: 1.62% in management fees, 2.16% in interest on borrowed money and 0.01% other. Without the interest, it's 1.63%.

To be fair, the interest cost is the price of the leverage that boosts the income, so it's not pure waste. But it's real money. On $100,000, $DVY's 0.38% is about $380 a year. $PDI's total expenses come to about $3,790. Every return number above is already after those costs.

Risk: how bumpy is the ride?

$DVY's risks: it's still a stock fund, so it falls in a bear market. It's heavy in banks and utilities and light on tech, so it lags badly when growth stocks lead. You can see that in the calendar years: iShares shows NAV returns of 31.63% in 2021, then just 1.92% in 2022 and 1.09% in 2023, before 16.19% in 2024 and 11.64% in 2025.

$PDI's risks: more of them, and they stack. Credit risk, because it owns lower quality mortgages, loans and high yield debt. Leverage risk, at about 37% of assets. Interest cost risk, since it pays to borrow. And premium and discount risk, which we just watched play out: the price fell 17% since June while the NAV fell about 9.5%. There's also distribution risk. A flat $0.2205 can't stay flat forever if the fund doesn't earn it.

None of that makes $PDI a bad fund. PIMCO is one of the best credit managers out there, and the long run NAV record shows it. It just means you need to understand what you're holding.

How I'd actually think about $DVY vs $PDI

I've been building dividend income for more than 10 years, and I don't think these two are really competitors. They do different jobs:

  • If you want income that can grow and a simple, cheap core holding: $DVY. No leverage, no premium games, a payout that has grown about 7% a year lately, and better 5 and 10 year total returns.
  • If you need high monthly cash flow now and understand credit risk: $PDI is one of the best known options, and buying at a discount instead of a premium tilts the odds in your favor. I'd keep it to a slice of an income portfolio, not the whole thing.
  • If you're chasing the 19% number: slow down. Look at the market price returns, not just the yield. That's the honest scorecard.

What I personally look at with any high yield fund: is the NAV holding up after the distributions are paid? If the NAV keeps sliding, part of that big yield is just your own money coming back. The takeaway

Over 10 years, $DVY returned 9.76% a year at NAV through September 30, 2026, versus 8.72% at NAV and 8.21% at market price for $PDI through June 30, 2026. Over 5 years the gap is wider: 9.68% versus 6.23% and 3.83%. $PDI pays roughly six times the income, around 19% at today's discounted price, but it uses 37% leverage, costs 3.79% a year all in, and its price has fallen 17% since June as its premium flipped to a discount. $DVY is the better total return compounder. $PDI is a high income tool for people who understand leveraged credit. Pick the one that matches the job.

FAQ

Is $DVY or $PDI better?
On total return, $DVY has been better over 5 and 10 years: 9.68% and 9.76% a year at NAV through September 30, 2026, versus 6.23% and 8.72% at NAV (3.83% and 8.21% at market price) for $PDI through June 30, 2026. $PDI pays far more income, around 19% at its November 18, 2026 price. Not financial advice.
What is the difference between BlackRock $DVY and PIMCO $PDI?
$DVY is an iShares by BlackRock index ETF holding about 100 high dividend U.S. stocks with no leverage and a 0.38% expense ratio. $PDI is an actively managed PIMCO closed-end fund holding mortgage credit, loans and bonds, with 37.35% leverage as of June 30, 2026 and estimated total expenses of 3.79% including interest.
How much does $PDI pay per month?
$PDI has paid $0.2205 per share every month in the record going back to April 2025. At the November 18, 2026 price of $13.86, that is a distribution rate of about 19.1%. The rate rose mainly because the price fell, not because the payment increased.
Why is $PDI trading at a discount?
$PDI is a closed-end fund, so its price can differ from its NAV. It traded at a 4.83% premium on June 30, 2026 and a 3.88% discount on November 18, 2026, as its NAV fell from $15.93 to $14.42 and demand for shares cooled.
Are $DVY and $PDI dividends qualified?
$DVY's dividends come from U.S. stocks and are mostly qualified. $PDI's distributions come mostly from bond and loan interest, which is generally taxed as ordinary income, and can include return of capital. Check your 1099 and a tax professional.
