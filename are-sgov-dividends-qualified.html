Are $SGOV Dividends Qualified? $SGOV Taxes and Total Return Explained (2026)

By Benjie · November 24, 2026 · 9 min read

Sponsored By IncomeBlastETFs.com

No, $SGOV dividends are not qualified dividends. $SGOV holds U.S. Treasury bills, so its monthly payouts are interest passed through to you, and they're taxed federally as ordinary income at your regular bracket. The good news: most of that income comes from U.S. government obligations, so it's generally exempt from state and local income tax. For 2025, iShares reported 95.14% of $SGOV's income came from U.S. government obligations.

"Is $SGOV qualified dividend" and "are $SGOV dividends qualified" keep showing up in the searches that bring people to this site. It's a fair question. Your broker calls the payments "dividends," they show up on a 1099-DIV, and $SGOV has quietly become one of the biggest ETFs in the market. So why doesn't it get the lower dividend tax rate?

Let's walk through it the way this site always does: what you actually keep. That means how $SGOV is taxed, the state tax break most people forget to claim, and what that does to your real total return.

Why $SGOV dividends aren't qualified

Qualified dividends are, in general, dividends from shares of U.S. corporations (and certain qualified foreign corporations) that you've held for a minimum holding period. That's what gets the lower 0%, 15% or 20% long-term capital gains rates.

$SGOV doesn't own any corporations. It tracks the ICE 0-3 Month US Treasury Securities Index, which means it owns Treasury bills that mature within three months. T-bills pay interest, not corporate dividends. When an ETF collects interest and passes it to you, the label says "dividend," but the tax character is still interest. That's why it lands in Box 1a (total ordinary dividends) on your 1099-DIV and not in Box 1b (qualified dividends).

You can see it on the iShares distribution table too. Every $SGOV payment over the last year is listed as 100% income, with zero short-term gains, long-term gains or return of capital.

The state tax break most people forget

Here's the part most people miss. Interest on U.S. Treasury securities is exempt from state and local income tax under federal law. When a fund like $SGOV passes that interest through, most states let you exclude the Treasury portion too.

The catch: your broker's 1099-DIV usually doesn't do this for you. It just shows one number in Box 1a. You have to look up the fund's U.S. government percentage and tell your tax software. iShares publishes it every year in its "U.S. Government Source Income Information" document, and the instructions are simple: multiply your Box 1a amount by the fund's percentage.

Why isn't it 100%? $SGOV holds a little cash and other short-term items for liquidity, and that slice of income doesn't count as Treasury interest. Still, 95% is about as clean as it gets.

The 50% rule in California, Connecticut and New York

A few states, including California, Connecticut and New York, only pass the exemption through if the fund kept at least 50% of its assets in federal obligations at the end of each quarter. If a fund misses that test, those states treat the exemption as 0%. iShares flags $SGOV (and $SHV) as meeting that 50% test for 2025, so investors in those states should be able to claim it. Always confirm your own state's rules.

How to claim it

  • Find your $SGOV dividends in Box 1a of your 1099-DIV (or the fund-level detail your broker includes).
  • Multiply by 95.14% for tax year 2025 (use the new percentage iShares publishes each year).
  • Enter that dollar amount as U.S. government interest on your state return. Most tax software asks about this right after you enter the 1099-DIV.
  • Check the state return for a "U.S. government interest" subtraction. If it's zero and you held $SGOV in a taxable account, something was missed.

What this means in real dollars

Say you have $100,000 in $SGOV in a taxable account. At the 3.62% trailing yield, that's about $3,620 a year in payouts. Here's my rough math on how the taxes break down:

  • Federal: all $3,620 is ordinary income. In a 22% bracket, that's about $796. If it were qualified at 15%, it would be about $543. So the "not qualified" part costs you around $250 a year at that bracket.
  • State: about $3,444 (95.14%) is Treasury income. In a state with a 9.3% rate, claiming the exemption saves roughly $320 a year. At a 5% state rate, it's about $172.

That state break is why I compare $SGOV to a bank CD or high-yield savings account on an after-tax basis, not just yield to yield. CD and savings interest is fully taxable by your state. In a 9.3% state, a CD would need to pay roughly 4.27% to match $SGOV's 3.89% SEC yield after state tax. In a 5% state, it's about 4.08%. Federal tax hits both the same way.

This is also why $SGOV is a great fit for an IRA or 401(k) if you're holding T-bills there anyway. Inside a tax-advantaged account, the qualified vs ordinary question doesn't matter at all.

Who $SGOV makes sense for

  • Cash you need in the next year or two: emergency fund, a house down payment, next year's expenses. $SGOV's 3 year standard deviation was 0.21%. It's about as close to cash as an ETF gets.
  • High tax state residents: the state exemption is real money compared to a CD or savings account. Just remember to claim it.
  • Dry powder: money waiting to go into stocks or income funds. A lot of income investors park cash in $SGOV and move it out over time.
  • Not for long-term growth: since inception, $SGOV has returned 2.97% a year. That's the price of almost zero risk. Money with a 10 year horizon usually belongs somewhere that can grow.

The takeaway

$SGOV dividends are not qualified. They're Treasury bill interest, taxed federally as ordinary income at your regular rate, and reported in Box 1a, not Box 1b. But about 95% of that income (95.14% for 2025) is from U.S. government obligations, which is generally exempt from state and local income tax, and iShares says $SGOV met the 50% asset test that California, Connecticut and New York require. Claim that exemption on your state return, compare $SGOV to CDs on an after-tax basis, and hold it in a retirement account if you can. Total return is basically the yield, so taxes are what decide how much you actually keep.

FAQ

Is $SGOV a qualified dividend?
No. $SGOV holds U.S. Treasury bills, so its distributions are interest income passed through to shareholders. They are reported as ordinary dividends in Box 1a of Form 1099-DIV, not as qualified dividends in Box 1b, and are taxed federally at ordinary income rates.
Are $SGOV dividends exempt from state tax?
Mostly. iShares reported that 95.14% of $SGOV's 2025 income came from U.S. government obligations, which is generally exempt from state and local income tax. You usually have to claim it yourself by multiplying your Box 1a amount by that percentage on your state return.
Does $SGOV meet the 50% rule for California, Connecticut and New York?
iShares flags $SGOV as having at least 50% of its assets in federal obligations at the end of each quarter of the 2025 fiscal year, which is the test those states use. Confirm your own state's rules or ask a tax professional.
Is $SGOV better than a CD after taxes?
It depends on rates and your state. Because CD interest is fully state taxable and most of $SGOV's income is not, a CD in a 9.3% state would need to pay roughly 4.27% to match $SGOV's 3.89% SEC yield (November 20, 2026) after state tax. Federal tax treats both as ordinary income.
How often does $SGOV pay dividends?
Monthly. Recent payments ranged from about $0.27 to $0.33 per share, with the November 2026 payment at $0.328581 per share, all classified as income.
