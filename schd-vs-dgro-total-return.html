$SCHD vs $DGRO: Total Return, Dividends and Which Is Better in 2026

By Benjie · November 12, 2026 · 10 min read

Sponsored By IncomeBlastETFs.com

Over 10 years, $DGRO has the better total return: 14.14% a year at NAV versus 13.70% for $SCHD, through October 31, 2026. That turns $10,000 into about $37,500 versus $36,100. $DGRO also wins the 3 and 5 year windows. But $SCHD crushed it over the last 12 months (38.54% vs 21.43%), and it pays a lot more income today, about 3.1% versus 1.9%.

"$SCHD vs $DGRO" is one of the matchups that keeps landing people on this site. I get why. They're two of the biggest, cheapest dividend ETFs out there, and they get recommended in the same breath constantly. If you own one, you've probably wondered about the other.

So let's settle it the way this site always does. Total return: price change plus every dividend, reinvested. Straight from Schwab and iShares.

Here's what jumps out at me. Over every multi-year window, $DGRO is ahead. Not by a mile, but consistently. Over 10 years it's about $1,400 ahead per $10,000 invested.

Then you look at 2026 and it's a totally different story. $SCHD is up 33.56% year to date versus 18.36% for $DGRO. A year ago, a lot of people were dumping $SCHD because it had lagged for so long. This is the part most people miss: the fund that looks "broken" on a 2 year chart can be the one that rips next.

So which one is better? Over the long haul, the record slightly favors $DGRO. Over the last year, it's not close. That's exactly why I judge on long windows, not on whatever just happened.

What's the difference between $SCHD and $DGRO?

Both are "dividend" ETFs, and both cost almost nothing. But the rules that decide what goes inside are very different.

$SCHD tracks the Dow Jones U.S. Dividend 100 Index. A company needs at least 10 straight years of paying dividends, then it gets scored on cash flow to debt, return on equity, dividend yield and 5 year dividend growth. The top 100 make it in. It's a quality screen with a real yield tilt.

$DGRO tracks the Morningstar US Dividend Growth Index. It wants at least 5 straight years of dividend growth, and it screens out companies whose payout ratios look stretched. Then it weights stocks by the dividends they actually pay. That's a much lower bar to get in, so it ends up with nearly 400 holdings, including big tech and bank names that pay a modest yield but keep growing it.

What's actually inside each fund

This is where the return gap comes from, and where the two funds really split.

$SCHD's top 10 is a cluster of roughly equal weights near 4% each: Texas Instruments (4.70%), Procter & Gamble (4.39%), Coca-Cola (4.28%), Home Depot (4.27%), Amgen (4.09%), Qualcomm (4.02%), PepsiCo (3.96%), UnitedHealth (3.90%), Chevron (3.89%) and ConocoPhillips (3.88%). That's about 41% of the fund in 10 names. By sector it leans on consumer staples (19.97%), health care (19.20%) and energy (15.38%), with just 9.14% in financials.

$DGRO is much flatter. Its biggest positions are Apple (3.10%), AbbVie (3.01%), Microsoft (3.00%), ExxonMobil (2.93%), Johnson & Johnson (2.82%), JPMorgan (2.67%), Broadcom (2.51%), Procter & Gamble (2.48%), Home Depot (2.18%) and Philip Morris (2.11%). The top 10 is about 27% of the fund. Financials are its biggest sector at 19.51%, followed by health care (17.76%) and technology (17.22%). Energy is only 5.25%.

So the short version: $SCHD is a concentrated bet on about 100 cash rich, higher yielding payers, heavy on staples, health care and energy. $DGRO is a broad dividend growth fund that owns Apple, Microsoft, Broadcom and the big banks. When tech and financials lead, $DGRO wins. When value, energy and defensive names lead, $SCHD wins. In 2026, it's been $SCHD's year.

The overlap is real but smaller than people think. Procter & Gamble and Home Depot sit in both top 10s, and plenty of the other names overlap further down. But $DGRO's top three (Apple, AbbVie, Microsoft) aren't anywhere near the top of $SCHD.

$SCHD vs $DGRO dividends: yield and dividend growth

On income today, $SCHD pays more. Schwab lists a 3.08% SEC yield and a 3.24% trailing distribution yield. iShares lists 1.94% and 1.84% for $DGRO. On $100,000 that's roughly $3,100 to $3,200 a year from $SCHD versus about $1,900 from $DGRO.

That's a big gap if you're living off the checks. It's a $1,200+ a year difference on every $100,000.

Fees: a rounding error

$SCHD costs 0.06% a year and $DGRO costs 0.08%. On a $100,000 position that's $60 versus $80. A $20 difference. Every return number above is already after fees. I would never pick between these two on cost.

Taxes

Both funds own U.S. stocks, so most of their dividends are generally qualified and taxed at the lower long term capital gains rates if you meet the holding period. Neither has a habit of paying out capital gains. $SCHD's higher yield means a bigger tax bill each year in a taxable account, which matters most while you're still building and reinvesting. If you're accumulating in a taxable account, $DGRO's lower yield is a small advantage. In an IRA or Roth, it doesn't matter.

How I'd actually think about $SCHD vs $DGRO

I've been a dividend investor for more than 10 years, and here's how I break this one down:

  • If you need income now: $SCHD. A yield above 3% versus under 2% is a real difference in the checks you get each quarter.
  • If you're still building and reinvesting: $DGRO has the slightly better long term total return record, a lower yield to pay taxes on, and much broader diversification, including tech.
  • If you want both: they actually pair well. $SCHD brings the yield and the value tilt. $DGRO brings Apple, Microsoft, Broadcom and the banks that $SCHD mostly skips. The overlap is manageable.

What I'd avoid is chasing the last 12 months. Anyone who swapped $SCHD for $DGRO in 2024 or early 2025 because $SCHD "stopped working" missed a huge 2026. And anyone piling into $SCHD now because of one great year should know $DGRO has won the 3, 5 and 10 year windows.

The risks on each side

$SCHD: concentration. About 41% in 10 stocks and big sector bets on staples, health care and energy. It reconstitutes once a year, and its 40.61% turnover is high for an index fund. With light tech exposure, it will lag in tech led markets, like it did in 2023 and 2024.

$DGRO: a lower yield that won't pay many bills on its own, and more exposure to big tech and banks, so it can move more with the broad market. Weighting by dividends paid also means the biggest payers carry the most weight, whether or not they're still growing fast.

The takeaway

Through October 31, 2026, $DGRO has the better total return over 3, 5 and 10 years (14.14% vs 13.70% a year over the decade), with more diversification and a tiny bit higher fee. $SCHD has the far better last 12 months, a much higher yield, and faster dividend growth over 2023 to 2025. If I wanted income today, the yield favors $SCHD. If I were still building and reinvesting, $DGRO's long record and broader portfolio are hard to argue with. Either way, read both prospectuses and do your own homework before you buy.
