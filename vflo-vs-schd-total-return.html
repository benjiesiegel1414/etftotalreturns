$VFLO vs $SCHD: Total Return, Dividends and Which Is Better in 2026

By Benjamin Siegel · November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Sponsored By IncomeBlastETFs.com

On total return, $VFLO has beaten $SCHD since it launched: about 28.3% a year versus 13.7% a year for $SCHD's much longer record, and 45.20% versus 38.50% over the past year, per StockAnalysis data in late October 2026. $SCHD wins on income, yield and fees: about 2.95% paid quarterly at a 0.06% expense ratio, versus roughly 1% at 0.39% for $VFLO. They're both "cash flow" funds, but they're built for different jobs.

"$VFLO vs $SCHD" is one of the matchups that keeps showing up in the searches that land on this site, along with "$VFLO vs $VOO". And I get why. $VFLO, the VictoryShares Free Cash Flow ETF, has quietly put up some of the best numbers of any large cap fund since 2023, and people want to know if it belongs next to the dividend fund everyone already owns.

So let's line them up on the number that actually settles it: total return. Price plus dividends. Then we'll talk about what's under the hood, because that's where the real decision is.

What's the difference between $VFLO and $SCHD?

This is the part most people miss. Both funds care about cash flow. But they use it for completely different things.

$SCHD: dividend payers with quality screens

$SCHD, the Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF, tracks the Dow Jones U.S. Dividend 100 Index. To even get in, a company needs a long record of paying dividends. Then it gets scored on things like cash flow to debt, return on equity, dividend yield and dividend growth. The result is about 100 mature, profitable companies. Top names right now include Texas Instruments, ConocoPhillips and Chevron.

The goal is income that grows. Cash flow is a filter for dividend safety.

$VFLO: free cash flow yield plus growth

$VFLO tracks the Victory US Large Cap Free Cash Flow Index. It doesn't care if a company pays a dividend at all. Here's how the index methodology builds it:

  • Start with large U.S. companies, excluding financials and REITs, that have positive free cash flow and positive earnings.
  • Take the 400 biggest by float market cap.
  • Keep the 75 with the highest free cash flow yield (free cash flow divided by enterprise value).
  • From those, keep the 50 with the best growth score, based on sales trend, EBITDA trend and long term EPS growth.
  • Weight them by free cash flow yield, with a 4% cap per stock and limits on any one sector. Rebalance quarterly.

In plain English: it's hunting for companies that are cheap relative to the cash they throw off, but still growing. Value with a growth filter. That's why Morningstar puts it in the mid cap value category even though it's a large cap index.

Look at that beta. $VFLO at 1.13 moves more than the market. $SCHD at 0.68 moves a lot less. That one number tells you a lot about what kind of ride each fund gives you.

$VFLO vs $SCHD dividends: which pays more?

$SCHD, and it isn't close.

  • $SCHD paid $1.05 per share over the last 12 months, about a 2.95% yield, in four quarterly payments.
  • $VFLO paid about $0.58 per share over the last 12 months, about a 1.06% yield. It pays monthly, but the checks bounce around a lot: $0.01343 in May 2026, $0.10576 in September and $0.09622 in October.

On a $100,000 position, that's roughly $2,950 a year from $SCHD versus about $1,060 from $VFLO. And $VFLO's monthly payment is not something I'd budget around. Some months it's a few cents a share, some months it's ten times that.

That's not a flaw. $VFLO just isn't an income fund. It owns companies that use their cash for buybacks, reinvestment and growth, not necessarily big dividends. $VFLO vs $VOO: is free cash flow beating the S&P 500?

So far, yes. Since $VFLO launched in June 2023 it's averaged about 28.3% a year versus 14.6% a year for $VOO over $VOO's much longer life. Over the last 12 months it was 45.20% versus 11.21%.

But they're very different portfolios. $VOO is about 500 stocks weighted by size, so the giant tech names drive it. $VFLO is 50 stocks weighted by cash flow yield, with a big chunk in energy and health care that barely moves the needle in the S&P 500. When mega cap tech leads, $VFLO can lag. When the market rotates toward cheaper, cash rich companies, it shines. The last year has been that second kind of market.

Why the lower yield won

This is the whole reason this site exists. Yield is not return.

$VFLO paid about a third of $SCHD's yield and still beat it on total return over the past year and since launch. All of that came from price growth. $VFLO traded around $54.56 in late October 2026, near the top of a 52 week range of $36.74 to $56.44.

The same lesson shows up everywhere on this site. In $JEPI vs $SCHD, the higher yield lost. Here, $SCHD is the higher yield, and it lost again. A big dividend tells you where the return comes from, not how much of it you get.

The tax angle

In a taxable account, a fund that returns most of its gain through price growth has a built in advantage: you don't pay tax on unrealized gains until you sell. $VFLO's small dividend means less taxable income each year. $SCHD's dividends are generally qualified, which is taxed at lower rates, but you still pay every year. Everyone's situation is different, so check with a tax professional.

How I'd actually think about $VFLO vs $SCHD

Here's what I actually look at: what job does this money have?

  • If you need income now, $SCHD. Higher yield, steadier quarterly checks, 15 years of history, and a 0.06% fee. $VFLO's tiny, lumpy monthly payout won't cover bills.
  • If you're building and reinvesting, $VFLO has been the stronger compounder so far. Just know you're paying 0.39% versus 0.06%, and you're betting on a three year track record. On $100,000, that's $390 a year versus $60.
  • If you want both, they actually pair pretty well. Different selection rules, different top holdings, different sector bets. $SCHD for the income base, $VFLO as a value tilt for growth.

Personally, I've always built around income I can count on. That's how I got to financial freedom. But I also think a lot of dividend investors ignore total return for too long. If a fund like $VFLO is compounding faster, it's worth understanding why, even if it never becomes the core of your income plan.

The risks

  • $VFLO's track record is short. Just over three years, mostly in a good market for its style. Expect it to look a lot worse in some years.
  • $VFLO is concentrated. 50 stocks, with about 23% in energy and about 30% in tech as of September 30. Oil prices and a few chip names can swing it hard.
  • $VFLO is more volatile. A beta of 1.13 means bigger moves than the market, in both directions.
  • $VFLO's fee is higher, and part of it is waived. The 0.39% net expense ratio reflects a fee waiver; the gross ratio is 0.44%. Check the current prospectus.
  • $SCHD leans on value sectors. When growth and tech lead, it can trail the S&P 500 for years.
  • Recent returns are not a forecast. A 45% year for $VFLO and a 38% year for $SCHD are unusual. Don't buy either one because of its last 12 months.

The takeaway

On total return, $VFLO has been the better fund since it launched in 2023, about 28.3% a year, and it beat $SCHD over the past year too, 45.20% versus 38.50%. $SCHD wins on income, cost and track record: about a 2.95% yield, a 0.06% fee and 15 years of data. If you need the dividends, $SCHD does the job. If you're compounding, $VFLO's free cash flow approach has earned a look. Either way, judge them on total return, not yield.
