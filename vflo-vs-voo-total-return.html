$VFLO vs $VOO: Total Return, Fees and Which Is Better in 2026

By Benjie · November 17, 2026 · 10 min read

Sponsored By IncomeBlastETFs.com

On total return, $VFLO has crushed $VOO over the last three years: 31.38% a year at NAV versus 23.37% for $VOO, through October 31, 2026. That turns $10,000 into about $22,700 versus $18,800. But almost all of that lead came in 2026. In both 2024 and 2025, plain old $VOO actually beat $VFLO, and it does it for 0.03% a year instead of 0.39%.

"$VFLO vs $VOO" keeps showing up in the searches that land on this site, and I get why. $VFLO, the VictoryShares Free Cash Flow ETF, has quietly become one of the best performing large cap funds in the market. And $VOO is the fund everybody already owns. So the real question is simple: is a 50 stock free cash flow fund actually better than just buying the S&P 500?

Let's settle it the way this site always does. Total return: price change plus every dividend, reinvested. Straight from Victory Capital and Vanguard.

Read that again. In the two full calendar years $VFLO has been around, $VOO won both. Not by a lot in 2025, but it won. The entire story of $VFLO "destroying the S&P 500" is mostly one year: 2026, when energy, health care and cheaper tech names ripped while the mega caps that dominate $VOO lagged.

That doesn't make $VFLO's run fake. It just tells you what kind of fund it is. It wins big when the market rotates toward cheap, cash rich companies. It trails, a little or a lot, when the biggest growth stocks lead.

What's the difference between $VFLO and $VOO?

$VOO is the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF. It owns the roughly 500 companies in the S&P 500, weighted by size. Bigger company, bigger slice. That's it. No opinions about value, cash flow or growth.

$VFLO tracks the Victory U.S. Large Cap Free Cash Flow Index, and it has a lot of opinions. Here's how Victory describes the build:

  • Start with the 400 largest profitable U.S. companies.
  • Keep the 75 with the highest free cash flow yield. That's free cash flow divided by enterprise value, using an average of the last 12 months and the next 12 months of expected cash flow.
  • From those, keep the 50 with the best growth score, based on sales trend, EBITDA trend and long term earnings growth estimates.
  • Weight them by free cash flow, cap any one stock at 4%, cap sectors, and rebalance every quarter.

In plain English: $VFLO hunts for companies that are cheap relative to the cash they throw off, but are still growing. Morningstar puts it in the mid cap value category, even though it owns big companies.

What's actually inside each fund

This is where the two really split.

$VFLO's top 10 as of November 17, 2026: Accenture (4.96%), Centene (3.73%), Marathon Petroleum (3.61%), Adobe (3.41%), ExxonMobil (3.40%), Intuit (3.39%), Micron (2.98%), Cigna (2.82%), EOG Resources (2.73%) and Phillips 66 (2.57%). That's about 34% of the fund in 10 names, and look at the mix: software and IT services, refiners and oil producers, and health insurers.

$VOO is the opposite shape. It's 506 stocks, but it's led by the trillion dollar tech giants, and Vanguard shows information technology alone at 37.4% of the fund.

Two things jump out. First, $VFLO has about 25% in energy versus under 4% in $VOO. That's a huge bet on oil and refining. Second, $VFLO owns zero banks and zero REITs. That's by design, since free cash flow doesn't really work as a measure for financial companies.

Valuation is the other big gap. Victory shows a trailing P/E of 16.15 for $VFLO's holdings. Vanguard shows 23.7x for $VOO. Different calculation methods, so don't read the decimals, but the direction is clear: you're paying a lot less per dollar of earnings in $VFLO.

So the short version: $VOO is a bet on the whole U.S. market, which today mostly means a bet on big tech. $VFLO is a concentrated bet on 50 cheaper, cash rich companies with a heavy energy and health care tilt. They overlap far less than people assume.

$VFLO vs $VOO dividends: does $VFLO pay monthly?

Sort of. $VFLO pays most months, but the amounts are all over the place. Over the last year it paid $0.150 in December 2025, nothing in January or February, $0.013 in May, $0.106 in September and $0.071 in November. Add up the payments from December 2025 through November 2026 and you get about $0.62 a share, roughly 1.2% at the November 16 NAV of $52.73. Victory lists a 0.99% SEC yield.

$VOO pays quarterly, and Vanguard lists a 1.02% SEC yield.

So on income, they're basically tied, both around 1%. On $100,000 you're looking at roughly $1,000 a year from either one. Neither of these is an income fund, and I wouldn't budget around $VFLO's lumpy checks. If income is your goal, that's a different shelf. Fees: 0.39% vs 0.03%

This is $VOO's clearest win. $VFLO charges 0.39% net, and that's after a fee waiver that runs through October 31, 2027. The gross ratio is 0.42%. $VOO charges 0.03%.

On $100,000 that's about $390 a year versus $30. Over 10 years, on a growing balance, that gap can easily run into the thousands. To be fair, every return number in this post is already after fees, and $VFLO has more than covered the difference so far. But the fee is guaranteed. The outperformance isn't.

Risk: how bumpy is the ride?

Victory shows $VFLO with a 3 year beta of 1.13 against the Russell 1000 Value Index and a standard deviation of 16.96%. Translation: it moves a bit more than its value benchmark, in both directions.

The bigger risk isn't the beta number. It's concentration. 50 stocks, about 25% in energy, and a quarterly rebalance that can shuffle the portfolio fast. If oil prices drop hard or health insurers hit another rough patch, $VFLO will feel it much more than $VOO.

And remember, $VFLO hasn't lived through a true bear market yet. $VOO has been through 2020 and 2022. Its 10 year record of 15.40% a year includes some real pain. $VFLO's entire record has been mostly a good market.

How I'd actually think about $VFLO vs $VOO

I've been investing for more than 10 years, and here's how I'd break this one down:

  • If you want one fund and never want to think about it: $VOO. 500 companies, 0.03%, and you will never trail the market because you are the market.
  • If you already own $VOO and want a value tilt: $VFLO is one of the more interesting ways to do it. Different sectors, different top holdings, a much lower P/E. It pairs well because it overlaps so little with the tech heavy top of the S&P 500.
  • If you're thinking of swapping $VOO for $VFLO because of the last 12 months: slow down. That's exactly the move I'd avoid.

Here's what I actually look at: what job does this money have? For me, core money should be cheap, broad and boring. A satellite position can be a factor bet like $VFLO. I'd never flip those roles because of one great year.

The risks on each side

$VFLO: a short track record (just over three years), concentration in 50 names, a big energy bet, no financials at all, a fee that's 13 times $VOO's, and a fee waiver that could change after October 2027. Its best stretch so far came in one unusual year.

$VOO: it's now very top heavy, with information technology at 37.4% of the fund and a median market cap of about $449 billion. When the tech giants stumble, $VOO stumbles with them, and 2026 showed how far it can fall behind a value tilted fund in a rotation year.

The takeaway

Through October 31, 2026, $VFLO beat $VOO by a mile on total return: 45.59% versus 12.07% over one year and 31.38% versus 23.37% a year over three years. But $VOO won both full calendar years of $VFLO's life, 2024 and 2025, and it costs 0.03% instead of 0.39%. Their yields are about the same. If I wanted a single core holding, the cost and simplicity favor $VOO. If I already owned the S&P 500 and wanted a cheaper, cash flow driven tilt, $VFLO has earned a look. Just don't buy it expecting another 45% year.

FAQ

Is $VFLO better than $VOO?
Recently, on total return, yes: through October 31, 2026 at NAV, $VFLO returned 45.59% over 1 year and 31.38% a year over 3 years, versus 12.07% and 23.37% for $VOO. But $VOO beat $VFLO in calendar 2024 and 2025, has a much longer record, and charges 0.03% versus 0.39%. Not financial advice.
What is the total return of $VFLO vs $VOO?
Through October 31, 2026 at NAV, $VFLO returned 39.28% year to date, 45.59% over 1 year, 31.38% a year over 3 years and 27.73% a year since its June 2023 launch. $VOO returned 11.73%, 12.07%, 23.37% and 15.40% a year over 10 years. $10,000 three years ago grew to about $22,700 in $VFLO versus $18,800 in $VOO.
Does $VFLO pay a monthly dividend?
$VFLO pays most months, but not every month and not in steady amounts. It skipped January and February 2026 and payments ranged from about $0.013 to $0.150 per share. The total from December 2025 through November 2026 was about $0.62 per share, roughly a 1% yield. $VOO pays quarterly with a 1.02% SEC yield.
What is the expense ratio of $VFLO vs $VOO?
$VFLO charges 0.39% net (0.42% gross, with a fee waiver through October 31, 2027). $VOO charges 0.03%. On $100,000 that is about $390 a year versus $30.
Can I own both $VFLO and $VOO?
Many investors pair a broad index fund with a factor tilt. $VFLO holds about 25% energy, 17% health care and no financials, while $VOO holds about 37% technology and 12% financials, so the overlap is smaller than you might expect. Not financial advice.
