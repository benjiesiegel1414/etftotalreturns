Covered Call ETF Total Returns Since Inception: $JEPI, $JEPQ, $QYLD, $SPYI and $QQQI (2026)

By Benjamin Siegel · October 19, 2026 · 8 min read

Sponsored By IncomeBlastETFs.com

Since inception, the big covered call ETFs have returned anywhere from about 8.9% a year ($QYLD) to about 19.9% a year ($QQQI), per each issuer's own fund page. $JEPQ is at 16.89% a year since May 2022, $SPYI at 14.73% since August 2022, and $JEPI at 10.81% since May 2020. The number that matters most isn't any of those on its own. It's how the total return compares to the yield the fund is paying you.

I've been investing for dividends for over 10 years, and this is the single comparison I wish more people made before buying a high yield ETF. A 12% yield means nothing if the fund only earns 9% a year in total. The difference comes out of your share price.

Why total return beats yield every time

Total return is simple: price change plus every distribution you received, assuming you reinvested them. It's the only number that tells you what actually happened to your money.

Here's the part most people miss. When an ETF pays out more than it earns in total return, the gap comes out of NAV. You get the check, but your shares are worth less. Do that for years and your income shrinks too, because the same yield on a smaller share price is a smaller paycheck.

So the quick check I run on every income ETF is this:

  • Total return above the yield: the fund is paying you and still growing. That's what you want.
  • Total return roughly equal to the yield: flat price, income only. Fine for some people, but there's no cushion.
  • Total return below the yield: you're being handed back part of your own capital. Over time, that usually shows up as price decay.

Fund by fund: what the numbers say

$QYLD: the cautionary tale

$QYLD has the longest track record here, over 12 years. Its since inception return is 8.87% a year at NAV, while its trailing 12 month distribution was 12.26%. That gap is the whole story. $QYLD sells at-the-money calls on the Nasdaq-100 every month, which maximizes premium but caps almost all of the upside. When the market rips, it barely participates. When the market drops, it takes most of the hit.

Look at the 1 year number though: 23.91% through August. Strong year, no question. That's why I always zoom out. One good year doesn't fix a 12 year pattern of paying out more than it earns. In our own data, $QYLD is flagged for price decay.

$JEPI: steady, but it lagged the market badly

$JEPI's 10.81% a year since May 2020 is above its 7.45% SEC yield, so it has earned its income. But compare it to the S&P 500, which J.P. Morgan lists at 17.72% a year over the same stretch. And the last 12 months were rough relatively speaking: 6.62% for $JEPI versus 15.74% for the S&P 500. That's the trade you make with a defensive, low volatility income fund. You give up a lot in strong markets.

$JEPQ: the Nasdaq version has done the heavy lifting

$JEPQ is at 16.89% a year since May 2022, comfortably above its 11.56% SEC yield. It still trailed the Nasdaq-100, which J.P. Morgan shows at 22.03% a year over the same period. That gap is the cost of the income. But in terms of "is the yield being earned," $JEPQ passes easily. If you want to dig into how it stacks up against $QYLD directly, our $JEPQ vs $QYLD comparison lines them up. For the full head to head with $JEPI, see my $JEPI vs $JEPQ total return breakdown.

$SPYI and $QQQI: the NEOS pair

$SPYI is at 14.73% a year since August 2022 (75.34% cumulative), above its 12.21% distribution rate. $QQQI is at 19.91% a year since January 2024 (62.42% cumulative), well above its 14.28% distribution rate. Both use index options that NEOS says are Section 1256 contracts, which get 60/40 tax treatment, and NEOS notes that distributions may be classified as return of capital. That's a big deal in a taxable account.

One caution on $QQQI: under three years of history, and almost all of it in a strong market for the Nasdaq-100. I like what I see so far, but I'd want to watch it through a real drawdown before calling it proven. See our $QQQI vs $SPYI matchup for the side by side.

How I actually use this

I don't pick income ETFs by yield. I pick them by total return first and then ask whether the yield fits my needs. Honestly, I'd rather own a 10% payer that grows than a 15% payer that slowly shrinks.

A few rules I stick to:

  • Compare since inception, not just 1 year. One great year can hide a long pattern of decay, like $QYLD shows.
  • Compare against the index the fund is built on. If a fund trails the plain index by a lot, make sure the income is worth it to you.
  • Be careful with short track records. Annualized returns on funds under 3 years old can swing a lot.
  • Watch the price chart too. If the share price keeps sliding while the yield stays high, that's your warning sign.

The takeaway

Covered call ETFs can be great income tools, but the headline yield is the least useful number on the page. Since inception, $QQQI, $JEPQ, $SPYI and $JEPI have all earned more in total return than they pay out. $QYLD has not, and its long history shows what that looks like over time.

Before you buy any high yield fund, check one thing: is the total return bigger than the yield? If yes, keep researching. If no, you're probably buying your own money back.

FAQ

What is $JEPQ's total return since inception?
J.P. Morgan reports $JEPQ's average annual total return at NAV as 16.89% from its May 3, 2022 inception through September 30, 2026, versus 22.03% a year for the Nasdaq-100 Index.
How does $JEPQ compare with $QYLD?
$JEPQ has returned 16.89% a year since May 2022 (as of September 30, 2026), while $QYLD has returned 8.87% a year since December 2013 (as of August 31, 2026). $JEPQ also charges less, 0.35% versus 0.60%. $QYLD writes at-the-money calls on the whole Nasdaq-100 every month, which caps more upside.
Is $SPYI or $QQQI better on total return?
Since inception $QQQI has the higher annualized return, 19.91% versus 14.73% for $SPYI as of September 30, 2026. $QQQI only launched in January 2024, so it has a much shorter track record.
What does ETF total return mean?
Total return is the change in the ETF's price plus all distributions paid, assuming they are reinvested. It shows what an investor actually earned, which is why it matters more than yield alone.
