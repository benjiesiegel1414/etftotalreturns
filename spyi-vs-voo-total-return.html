One pays you a fat monthly check. The other barely pays anything and just compounds. So which one actually made you more money?

That's the only question this site cares about. Total return. Price change plus every distribution reinvested. Let's look at the real numbers from NEOS and Vanguard.

What's the difference between $SPYI and $VOO?

Same stocks underneath. Very different machines.

$VOO: just the S&P 500, as cheap as it gets

$VOO, the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF, launched September 7, 2010. It holds all the S&P 500 companies (505 stocks as of September 30, 2026) weighted by size, and charges 0.03% a year. That's $3 a year on $10,000. It pays a small dividend every quarter and lets the rest compound. No options, no tricks.

$SPYI: the S&P 500 plus a call option strategy

$SPYI, the NEOS S&P 500 High Income ETF, launched August 29, 2022. It owns the stocks in the S&P 500 and then sells SPX index call options against the portfolio to generate income, sometimes buying calls too, which NEOS says can leave room for some upside in rising markets. It's actively managed, pays monthly, and charges 0.68% a year. That's $68 a year on $10,000.

The holdings look almost the same. $SPYI's top holdings on November 9, 2026 were $AAPL (7.79%), $NVDA (7.07%), $MSFT (6.14%), $AMZN (3.97%) and $GOOGL (2.59%), per NEOS. $VOO's biggest at the end of September were $NVDA (8.38%), $AAPL and $MSFT, per Vanguard. So you're not choosing different companies. You're choosing what happens to the upside.

Where the return gap comes from

Here's the simplest way I can put it. When you sell a call option, you trade some of the stock market's future upside for cash today. That cash is your monthly check. In a big up market, you gave away more upside than the premium was worth. In a flat or choppy market, you kept the premium and didn't give up much. That's the whole trade.

Look at the share prices since $SPYI's first day of trading:

  • $VOO closed at about $366.03 on August 30, 2022. On November 6, 2026 it closed at $675.99. That's up about 85% on price alone, before dividends.
  • $SPYI closed at about $49.13 on August 30, 2022. On November 6, 2026 it closed at $51.29. That's up about 4%.

Closing market prices from Yahoo Finance.

So nearly all of $SPYI's return has come through the monthly checks, while the share price has mostly held flat. The good news: it held. The share price hasn't melted like some high income funds I've written about. It just didn't grow much, because the options sold off most of the upside in a market that went straight up.

That also explains the last 12 months. The S&P 500's gain over that stretch was smaller and bumpier ($VOO hit a 52 week low on March 30, 2026 before recovering, per Vanguard), and in that kind of market, collecting premium every month keeps you close to the index. That's when $SPYI shines. In big trending years like 2023 and 2024, when $VOO returned 26.25% and 24.98% per Vanguard, that's where the gap opens up.

One more thing in $SPYI's favor: it beat the plain buy write approach. Over 3 years, NEOS shows $SPYI at 17.98% a year versus 14.97% for the Cboe S&P 500 BuyWrite Monthly Index, and 14.29% versus 11.74% since inception. So as call option funds go, it has done its job. It just hasn't beaten owning the index outright, and very few option income funds ever do over a full bull market.

$SPYI vs $VOO dividends: the income gap

This is why anyone looks at $SPYI in the first place.

  • $SPYI pays every month. In 2026 it paid between $0.5087 and $0.5423 per share each month: $0.5309 in January, $0.5423 in August, $0.5338 in September and $0.5087 in October, per NEOS. Next ex-date is November 18, 2026.
  • $VOO pays every quarter. Its last four dividends were $1.7710, $1.8724, $1.9622 and $1.8226 per share, per Vanguard, about $7.43 a year on a $676 share.

On a $100,000 position, that's roughly:

  • $SPYI at 11.43%: about $11,430 a year, or about $950 a month
  • $VOO at about 1.1%: about $1,100 a year, or about $90 a month

That's a huge difference in cash flow. If you're living off your portfolio, $SPYI does in $100,000 what $VOO would need around a million dollars to do in dividends alone.

The index fund crowd will say: just sell 4% of your $VOO every year and make your own paycheck. Mathematically, with a higher total return, that has worked better over this stretch. I just know how I think about it. I reached financial freedom at 31 by building income that shows up on its own. I'd rather not decide how many shares to sell every month, especially in a year when the market is down 20%. That peace of mind is worth something, but it's not free. The tax angle

This is where $SPYI gets interesting for a taxable account.

  • Return of capital. NEOS states that $SPYI's distributions have been classified as return of capital. ROC generally isn't taxed when you receive it. It lowers your cost basis instead, so the tax shows up later when you sell.
  • Section 1256 options. NEOS says the SPX index options $SPYI uses are Section 1256 contracts, taxed 60% long term and 40% short term no matter how long they were held.
  • $VOO's dividends are small and mostly qualified, so the tax bill on them is low. Most of $VOO's return is unrealized price growth, which isn't taxed until you sell.

Return of capital isn't automatically good or bad. It's a tax label, not a performance score. Everyone's situation is different, so talk to a tax professional before you build a plan around it.

How I'd actually think about $SPYI vs $VOO

Here's what I actually look at: what job does this money have?

  • Long runway and no need for income? $VOO. Higher total return over 3 years and since $SPYI launched, a fee that's basically zero, and no option strategy capping your upside. Hard to beat for an accumulator.
  • Need monthly income now? $SPYI pays about ten times more cash, monthly, with a share price that has held roughly flat and a tax setup that's friendly for taxable accounts.
  • Somewhere in between? Plenty of investors own both: $VOO for growth, $SPYI for a monthly paycheck. They own the same stocks, so you aren't adding diversification, you're choosing how much upside to trade for income.
  • Reinvesting every payout? Then the income is just a different route to the same place, and the total return table says $VOO has been the faster route. Think hard about whether you need the option strategy at all.

Personally, I'd never call $SPYI a replacement for $VOO. It's a different tool. Yield is what you get paid. Total return is what you actually earned. You can line up any two funds yourself with our ETF total return comparison tool.

The risks on each side

  • $SPYI's capped upside. In strong bull markets it will lag the S&P 500, sometimes by a lot. That gap compounds.
  • $SPYI's payouts move. Option premiums depend on volatility. The monthly check slid from $0.5423 in August to $0.5087 in October 2026. Plan around a range, not your best month.
  • $SPYI's short history. About four years, and it hasn't lived through a long bear market yet.
  • $SPYI's fee. 0.68% versus 0.03% is a real drag over decades.
  • $VOO's concentration. At the end of September, information technology was about 40% of $VOO and the top 10 holdings about 41%, per Vanguard. $SPYI holds the same giants, so it shares this risk too.
  • $VOO's tiny income. If you need cash, you'll be selling shares, and selling in a crash is painful.

The takeaway

On total return, $VOO has been the better fund over the same dates: about 17.9% a year versus 14.29% for $SPYI since $SPYI launched in August 2022, and 23.37% versus 17.98% a year over 3 years. Over the last 12 months they were essentially tied, and $SPYI pays roughly 11% a year in monthly income versus about 1.1% for $VOO. If you're building wealth and don't need the cash, the record favors $VOO. If you need a monthly paycheck from the S&P 500, $SPYI has delivered it without its share price falling apart. Either way, read both prospectuses and do your own homework before you buy.

FAQ

Is $SPYI better than $VOO?
On total return, $VOO has been better over the same period: about 17.9% a year versus 14.29% for $SPYI from August 29, 2022 through October 31, 2026, and 23.37% versus 17.98% a year over 3 years. Over the last 12 months they were nearly tied (12.07% vs 11.91%). $SPYI pays far more income, an 11.43% distribution rate versus about 1.1% for $VOO. Which is better depends on whether you need income or maximum growth.
What is the total return of $SPYI vs $VOO?
Through October 31, 2026 at NAV, $SPYI returned 11.91% over 1 year, 17.98% a year over 3 years and 74.59% cumulative since its August 2022 launch. $VOO returned 12.07% over 1 year and 23.37% a year over 3 years, and the S&P 500 it tracks returned 99.38% cumulative since $SPYI's launch.
How much does $SPYI pay per month compared to $VOO?
$SPYI paid between $0.5087 and $0.5423 per share each month in 2026, about $950 a month on $100,000 at its 11.43% distribution rate. $VOO pays quarterly, about $7.43 per share a year, roughly $1,100 a year on $100,000.
Can $SPYI replace $VOO?
Not as a like for like swap. Both own the S&P 500, but $SPYI sells call options that trade away much of the upside for monthly income, and it charges 0.68% versus 0.03%. It has trailed the S&P 500 over every multi-year window so far. It's an income tool, not a growth substitute. Not financial advice.
Should I own both $SPYI and $VOO?
Some investors pair them, using $VOO for growth and $SPYI for monthly income. Keep in mind they hold the same companies, so owning both doesn't add diversification. It changes how much of the market's upside you trade for cash today.
