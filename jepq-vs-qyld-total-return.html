$JEPQ vs $QYLD: Total Return, Dividends and Which Is Better in 2026

By Benjie · November 6, 2026 · 10 min read

Sponsored By IncomeBlastETFs.com

Over the long run, $JEPQ has the better total return: about 15.2% a year at NAV since it launched in May 2022, versus roughly 10.6% a year for $QYLD over the same stretch. $JEPQ also wins over 3 years (20.04% vs 15.78% a year) and is cheaper at 0.35% vs 0.60%. But over the last 12 months through October 31, 2026, $QYLD actually came out ahead, 15.64% to 9.49%. Here's why both of those things are true, and what I'd do with it.

"How does $JEPQ compare with $QYLD?" is one of the questions that keeps sending people to this site, along with "is there an alternative to $QYLD for covered call income?" Makes sense. They're the two big Nasdaq-100 income funds. Both pay monthly. Both yield in the double digits. And on the surface they look like the same product.

They're not. The way each one sells options is completely different, and that difference shows up in the one number that matters on this site: total return. Price plus dividends.

On $10,000 invested when $JEPQ launched in May 2022, that's roughly $18,890 in $JEPQ by the end of October 2026 versus roughly $15,700 in $QYLD. That's a big gap for two funds that look like twins on a yield screener.

And notice the pattern. In 2023, the Nasdaq-100 ran 55%. $JEPQ kept 36% of it. $QYLD kept about 23%. In strong up years, $JEPQ keeps a lot more of the upside. That's the whole ballgame over a full cycle.

What's the difference between $JEPQ and $QYLD?

This is the part most people miss. Both funds sell call options on the Nasdaq-100. But they do it in very different ways.

$QYLD: at-the-money calls on the whole portfolio

$QYLD, the Global X Nasdaq 100 Covered Call ETF, tracks the Cboe Nasdaq-100 BuyWrite V2 Index. Every month it owns the Nasdaq-100 stocks and sells one-month at-the-money index calls against them. On November 5, 2026, its short call position was about $7.69 billion against $7.74 billion in net assets. Basically the whole fund is covered.

At-the-money calls pay the biggest premium. The tradeoff: if the Nasdaq goes up, almost all of that gain belongs to whoever bought the call. If the Nasdaq goes down, $QYLD takes most of the hit, cushioned only by the premium. Limited upside, most of the downside. Over time, that math grinds the share price lower.

$JEPQ: active stock picking plus options through ELNs

$JEPQ, the JPMorgan Nasdaq Equity Premium Income ETF, runs an actively managed portfolio made up largely of Nasdaq-100 stocks. J.P. Morgan describes the stock side as "data science enabled." The income comes from selling Nasdaq-100 call options through equity-linked notes (ELNs), and the prospectus caps ELNs at 20% of net assets.

In plain English: $JEPQ isn't mechanically selling at-the-money calls on 100% of the portfolio every month. That leaves more room for the stocks to actually go up, which is exactly what you see in the 2023 and 2024 numbers.

$JEPQ vs $QYLD dividends: which pays more?

On yield, $QYLD. On dollars and consistency, it's closer than you'd think.

  • $QYLD pays almost the same amount every month. Recent payouts: $0.1785 (May), $0.1854 (June), $0.1775 (July), $0.1829 (August), $0.1767 (September) and $0.1826 (October 2026). Its trailing 12 month yield was 13.44% on November 5.
  • $JEPQ moves around more because the option premium it collects depends on market volatility. Recent payouts: $0.56444 (June), $0.63658 (July), $0.70497 (August), $0.68255 (September), $0.56687 (October) and $0.53922 (November 2026). Its trailing 12 month yield was 11.87%.

On a $100,000 position, that's very roughly $13,000 to $13,400 a year from $QYLD versus about $11,900 from $JEPQ at current yields. So yes, $QYLD pays a bit more cash.

But look at what you're getting paid with. Global X estimated that 98.76% of $QYLD's September 2026 distribution was return of capital. That's a tax classification, not proof the fund is "giving you your own money back," but combined with the price chart below, it tells you the payout isn't coming from growth.

The price decay problem with $QYLD

This is the chart I always come back to.

  • $QYLD's first close in December 2013 was about $25.04. On November 4, 2026 it closed at $17.98. That's down about 28% on price over almost 13 years, during one of the best runs the Nasdaq has ever had.
  • Since $JEPQ launched in May 2022, $QYLD went from about $19.65 to $17.98. Down about 8.5%.
  • $JEPQ over the same stretch went from a first close of about $51.10 to $57.11. Up about 12%, while paying double-digit yields the whole way.

Closing prices from Yahoo Finance. On November 5, 2026 both funds fell about 6% in one day ($QYLD closed at $16.88, $JEPQ at $53.71), so I used November 4 closes to avoid one bad day skewing the long-run picture.

Here's why that matters to an income investor. If the share price keeps sliding, your future income slides with it, because the same yield on a smaller price is a smaller check. A flat or rising share price is what keeps your income growing. That's how I've always thought about it: I want the income machine to hold its value, not slowly eat itself.

So why did $QYLD win the last 12 months?

Fair question, and I don't want to hide it. Through October 31, 2026, $QYLD returned 15.64% versus 9.49% for $JEPQ. Through September 30, it was 22.94% versus 19.45%.

At-the-money call writing does best when the Nasdaq chops around or grinds sideways with high volatility. You collect fat premiums and don't give up much upside, because there wasn't much upside to give up. $JEPQ's active stock picks also matter here; when its holdings lag the index, it shows.

One good year doesn't change the pattern, though. Over 3 years, over each of the last three calendar years, and since $JEPQ launched, $JEPQ has the higher total return. I always zoom out before I decide anything.

Is there an alternative to $QYLD for covered call income?

Yes, and $JEPQ is the obvious one if you want Nasdaq-100 exposure. A few others people compare:

  • $QQQI (NEOS Nasdaq-100 High Income) also writes calls on the Nasdaq-100 and has posted strong total returns since 2024. I compared it in $SPYI vs $QQQI total return.
  • $JEPI is $JEPQ's S&P 500 sibling: lower yield, lower volatility. See $JEPI vs $JEPQ total return.

If you want to browse more monthly payers, MonthlyETFs.com lists them, and TopDividendETFs.com tracks yield and price decay across the high income ETF universe.

The tax angle

Both funds pay most of their return as distributions, which is less tax efficient in a regular brokerage account. $JEPQ's own prospectus shows the drag: in 2025 it returned 15.19% before taxes but 10.20% after taxes on distributions. $QYLD's distributions have been estimated as mostly return of capital, which lowers your cost basis instead of being taxed right away. Everyone's situation is different, so check with a tax professional. A lot of people hold funds like these in an IRA for exactly this reason.

How I'd actually think about $JEPQ vs $QYLD

Here's what I actually look at: what job does this money have, and will the income machine still be intact in 10 years?

  • If you want Nasdaq income and some growth, $JEPQ has the better record. Higher total return over every multi-year window, a lower fee, and a share price that has held up.
  • If you only care about the biggest, steadiest monthly check right now, $QYLD pays a little more and the amount barely changes month to month. Just go in knowing the price has historically drifted lower, so that check has been shrinking over time.
  • If you're reinvesting and years away from living off it, honestly, compare both against plain growth or dividend growth funds first. Our ETF total return comparison tool lets you line up any two funds.

Personally, I'd rather own the fund that keeps more of the upside than chase an extra point or two of yield. Yield is not return. That's the whole reason this site exists.

The risks

  • Both are tied to the Nasdaq-100. Heavy tech concentration. November 5, 2026 was a reminder: both funds fell about 6% in a single day.
  • Capped upside. Both give up gains in strong markets. Neither will keep pace with $QQQ in a big up year.
  • $QYLD's price decay. Its share price has trended lower over its life, which shrinks future income.
  • $JEPQ's payouts vary. When volatility drops, premiums drop, and so do the monthly checks.
  • $JEPQ is active and uses ELNs. Results depend on J.P. Morgan's stock picks, and ELNs carry counterparty risk.
  • Short history for $JEPQ. About four and a half years, versus almost 13 for $QYLD.

The takeaway

On total return, $JEPQ has been the better Nasdaq-100 income fund since it launched in 2022: about 15.2% a year versus roughly 10.6% for $QYLD, with a lower 0.35% fee and a share price that has held up. $QYLD pays slightly more cash and had the better last 12 months, but its long-run price decay is real. If you're choosing between the two for the long haul, the total return record favors $JEPQ. Either way, do your own homework and read both prospectuses before you buy.

FAQ

How does $JEPQ compare with $QYLD?
Both are monthly-paying Nasdaq-100 covered call ETFs. $JEPQ has the higher total return over 3 years (20.04% vs 15.78% a year through October 31, 2026) and since its May 2022 launch, and costs 0.35% vs 0.60%. $QYLD has a slightly higher yield and returned more over the last 12 months (15.64% vs 9.49%).
Is there an alternative to $QYLD for covered call income?
Yes. $JEPQ is the most common Nasdaq-100 alternative, and $QQQI is another. Both have kept more of the Nasdaq's upside than $QYLD's at-the-money approach. $JEPI and $SPYI are S&P 500 based options. This is not a recommendation.
Which pays more, $JEPQ or $QYLD?
$QYLD has the higher yield, about 13.44% trailing 12 months versus 11.87% for $JEPQ as of November 5, 2026. $QYLD pays roughly $0.18 per share every month, while $JEPQ's monthly payout varies, between about $0.54 and $0.70 per share from June to November 2026.
Why does $QYLD's price keep going down?
$QYLD sells at-the-money calls on essentially its whole portfolio every month. It gives up most of the upside when the Nasdaq rises but keeps most of the downside when it falls. Over time that has pushed the share price from about $25 at its 2013 launch to about $18 in November 2026.
Is $JEPQ better than $QYLD?
On long-run total return, $JEPQ has been better: about 15.2% a year at NAV since May 2022 versus roughly 10.6% for $QYLD over the same period. Which is better for you depends on whether you prioritize the highest current yield or total return. Not financial advice.
