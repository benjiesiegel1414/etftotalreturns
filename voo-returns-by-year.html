$VOO Returns by Year: Every Annual Total Return Since 2011 (2026)

By Benjie · November 26, 2026 · 9 min read

Sponsored By IncomeBlastETFs.com

$VOO has returned between -18.15% and +32.33% in a single calendar year since it launched, and it averaged about 14.0% a year from 2011 through 2025. Over the last 10 full years (2016 to 2025) it compounded at roughly 14.8% a year, turning $10,000 into about $39,700. Here is every annual return straight from Vanguard, split into price gains and dividends, plus what the numbers actually mean for you.

"$VOO returns by year," "$VOO average return last 10 years" and "$VOO annual returns since inception" keep showing up in the searches that bring people to this site. Makes sense. $VOO is close to a trillion dollar fund now, and it's the yardstick almost every income ETF gets measured against, including on this site.

So I pulled Vanguard's own calendar year data and laid it out the way I like to look at it: total return first, because that's what you actually keep.

A few things jump out right away:

  • 13 of 15 years were positive. The only down years were 2018 (-4.42%) and 2022 (-18.15%).
  • The best year was 2013 at +32.33%, with 2019 (+31.46%) right behind it.
  • 6 of the 15 years beat 20%, and 11 beat 10%.
  • 2011 and 2015 were flat on price. The only reason they show a gain at all is the dividend. That's a nice reminder that the income piece is doing real work even on a "growth" fund.

$VOO average return: 5, 10 and 15 years

"Average return" can mean two different things, and this is the part most people miss. The simple average of the 15 yearly numbers above is about 14.9%. But the number you actually earned, the compound annual return, is about 14.0%. The gap comes from volatility: a -18% year hurts more than a +18% year helps.

So if someone asks "what's $VOO's average return over the last 10 years," the honest answer is about 15% a year, depending on exactly which 10 years you pick. Through October 31, 2026, Vanguard shows 15.40%. Using full calendar years 2016 to 2025, I get 14.78%.

$VOO return so far in 2026

Year to date through October 31, 2026, $VOO was up 11.73% at NAV, per Vanguard. The ride hasn't been smooth. The first quarter was -4.34%, then the second quarter bounced +15.19%, and the third quarter added +2.30%. Vanguard shows October at -0.87%.

That pattern is pretty normal for the S&P 500. Even in the good years, there's usually at least one quarter that makes people nervous. In 2020, the first quarter was -19.63% and the fund still finished the year up 18.35%.

Price return vs dividend return: why total return matters

This is the whole reason this site exists. Look at the price column alone and you'd think $10,000 in 2011 became about $54,500 by the end of 2025. Add the dividends back in, reinvested, and it's about $71,600. That's roughly $17,000 of difference from a fund that "only" yields about 1.1% today.

$VOO's dividend return has drifted down over time, from about 2.1% to 2.7% a year in the early 2010s to 1.43% in 2025. Prices went up faster than dividends, so the yield shrank. That doesn't mean the income got worse in dollars. It means the price did a lot of the heavy lifting.

Here's how I think about it as an income investor: $VOO isn't built to pay your bills today. At a 1.1% yield, $100,000 pays about $1,100 a year. But it's the benchmark I hold every high yield fund up against. If an income ETF pays 12% but its total return trails $VOO by a mile, I'm paying for that big check with my principal.

What these numbers don't tell you

  • The last 15 years were unusually good. The long-run S&P 500 average is often quoted closer to 10% a year. Planning around 14% to 15% forever is how people get disappointed.
  • $VOO launched right after the 2008 crash. It has never been through a full decade like 2000 to 2009, when the S&P 500 went roughly nowhere. Its track record starts near a low point.
  • Taxes aren't in these numbers. Vanguard shows a 10 year after-tax return (on distributions) of 14.83% as of September 30, 2026, vs 15.29% before tax. $VOO is very tax efficient, but not tax free in a taxable account.
  • Concentration is higher than it used to be. The biggest tech companies make up a large share of the index now, so $VOO is less "diversified" than the 500 stock count suggests.

Who $VOO makes sense for

  • Long-term builders: if you're still in the accumulation phase and won't touch the money for 10+ years, a 0.03% expense ratio S&P 500 fund is hard to beat.
  • A core next to income funds: a lot of dividend investors pair $VOO with higher yield funds so the portfolio still grows while it pays.
  • Not for income today: at about 1.1%, it won't cover many bills. If you want monthly checks, browse MonthlyETFs.com, and run any fund through DividendProjection.com to see what it could pay you year by year.

The takeaway

$VOO's annual returns since 2011 range from -18.15% (2022) to +32.33% (2013), with only two down years out of fifteen. It compounded at about 14.0% a year over those 15 years and about 14.8% over the last 10 calendar years, and Vanguard shows 15.40% a year for the 10 years ending October 31, 2026. Dividends were a quiet but real part of that, adding roughly 1.3% to 2.7% each year. Just don't assume the next 15 years will look like the last 15. Use $VOO as your benchmark, and judge every income ETF by whether its total return can keep up.
